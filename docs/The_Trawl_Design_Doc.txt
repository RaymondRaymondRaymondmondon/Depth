  Overview and pillars
  The Trawl is a 1–6 player co-op fishing horror game: a crew works a steam trawler through
  three nights per deadline to meet the Owners' quota, while a living food web under the
  hull decides what bites, what hunts, and what comes up the rail. It replaces the Trawl
  section of the Depth Master Reference (1–4 players, one stub loop) and becomes the full
  design Claude Code builds from, the way Fathoms has its own document.

  What it takes from each source. From Lethal Company: the quota and deadline, a shared
  ship, scavenging for value, a store and a buy-back rate that changes by day, monsters with
  tells and counters, a terminal, proximity voice, and dead players who stay useful. From
  How to Fish: a real cast-and-reel with line tension, rod and reel stats, lures and bait that
  matter per species, fishing grounds with their own catch tables, a collection log, and gear
  progression that opens new water.

  Pillars

   1. The sea is simulated, not scripted. Fish, predators, and threats are agents in one food
      web. A monster shows up because something drew it: blood in the water, light, noise, a
      school it hunts.
   2. Every catch is a physics fight. Line, rod, fish, and boat are one connected system. A
      marlin can drag the boat; a snagged net can heel it over.
   3. Greed is the enemy. Brighter lanterns, bigger nets, chum, and deeper water all pay
      more and all attract what kills you. The crew decides every night how far to push.
   4. Six people, one boat, too many jobs. There are always more stations than hands, so
      talking, trading jobs, and trusting each other is the game.
   5. Dread is readable. Every threat has a sound tell, a visual tell, a sonar tell, and a counter.
      Death is always explainable afterward.

  Scope rules. Everything stays procedural (no image or audio files). The deck is top-down
  pixel art from the parkour pipeline; underwater views (sonar, dive, reel camera) use the
  same pixel palette. Numbers live in trawl_data.cpp . The host runs the simulation; clients
  send commands. Every system ships with a headless self-test on depth.exe .

  The session loop
  A run is a string of deadlines. Each deadline is three nights at sea; a session (one deadline)
  takes about 30–35 minutes with six players. Meet the quota and the next deadline starts
  with a higher one; miss it and the Owners repossess the boat and the run ends.

  The Owners. An unseen shipping concern that rents the crew the Gannet, a 22 m steam
  trawler, and licenses it to fish only at night. They speak through a brass telegraph in the
  wheelhouse (the Lethal Company terminal and Company voice in one). Their messages
  are terse, occasionally wrong, and never sympathetic.

  The telegraph. The Owners speak only by brass telegraph: a tape clatters out of the
  wheelhouse printer in capitals, with STOP for every full stop, and a bell tick every
  crewman hears. Their voice is mercantile, polite in form, indifferent in fact; they care
  about the hold and the boat, never the hands. Everything the Owners say is printed on
  tape that can be torn off and read later on the crew board. About 30% of rumors are
  wrong, and the Owners never admit it.

  The telegraph as a tell. On the Kraken's Glass, the tape prints only dots. When the Ghost
  Ship is near, it prints a message from a boat that sank years ago. Neither is ever explained.

  One deadline

   1. Dock (untimed, 2–4 min). The chalkboard shows the quota, nights left, and money. The
      crew sells last night's catch, buys gear at the Chandler, refits the boat at the Slipway,
       and loads bait, ice, and coal.
   2. Choose a ground. The helm picks one of the four grounds on the chart table. Each
      costs coal to reach, and the telegraph posts that night's conditions: weather, moon
      phase, and a rumor ("Whalers report the Weeds quiet"; rumors are right 70% of the
      time).
   3. Sail out (about 45 s). A short top-down run through the harbor mouth and fog. The
      engine crew brings up steam; the helm avoids buoys and rocks. Anything left
      unsecured on deck slides.
   4. The night (9 real minutes, 20:00 to 05:00 on the wheelhouse clock). The crew fishes,
      trawls, shoots, dives, and survives. The sea starts calm and wakes as the night goes on
       and as the crew makes blood, noise, and light.
   5. Run for harbor. The crew decides when to leave. The return takes 45–90 s depending
      on distance and steam. The Gannet must cross the harbor line before 05:00; after that,
      the Owners' customs cutter seizes the hold and fines 10% of money. There is always a
      reason to stay ten more minutes.
   6. Sell. Fish go to the Fish Market (paid now, price set by freshness and glut). Salvage
      goes to the Owners (paid at a rate that rises toward the deadline, see Economy).
      Money past the quota buys gear.
   7. Repeat for nights two and three. After the third night's sale, the Owners count the
      quota.

  Quota. Starts at 400 shillings for six players (scaled 0.5× solo, 0.7× for two, 0.85× for four;
  bots count as crew). After each met deadline it rises by 40% plus 60 shillings, so by the
  fourth deadline the crew must work Atlantis Waters or dive for relics to keep up. Surplus
  money carries over; the quota does not reset.

  The ground remembers. A ground's ecosystem state (populations, hunger, blood, and any
  Wake the crew raised) persists across the three nights of a deadline. Emptying the Weeds
  of sardines on night one means hungrier, bolder predators there on night two. Grounds
  reset when a new deadline starts.

  Night pacing (the Stir clock). Each ground has a baseline threat curve that rises through
  the night, plus whatever the crew adds. The first two minutes are almost always safe; the
  last two minutes of an ordinary night have a large threat on the water about half the time.


  First night: the shakedown
  The first time a lobby includes a player with no completed nights, the host is offered a
  shakedown night: a 6-minute night on Eclipse Lagoon with a fixed seed, no quota, and no
  deaths that count. It can be skipped, and any crew can replay it from the arcade menu.
  The only guide is Kess, an old deckhand who is aboard for this night only and talks in short
  chalked lines on the deck ("Line's slack. Reel.").

   1. The deck. Walk to each station; take the handline; catch three silversides for the bait
       well.
   2. The rod. Bait a light rod with a silverside, cast at the marked snapper school, set the
      hook on the take, and land a snapper with side pressure. Kess gaffs it if the angler asks
       with a ping.
   3. The table. Gut and ice the snapper; Kess throws the guts over the side.
   4. The sonar. Ping, mark the sardine ball, and steer onto it.
   5. The net. Shoot the trawl through the ball and haul it with two hands; sort the moon
      jellies back over the side.
   6. Blood. The guts from step 3 bring a reef shark. It takes a hooked fish, then circles. The
       crew learns the counters: stop the blood, get off the rail, gaff or cannon, or steam away.
   7. Overboard. Kess slips on the wet deck and goes in. Stop the screw and throw the ring.
   8. Home. The clock runs to 05:00 fast; cross the harbour line, carry the catch to the Fish
      Market, and read the chalkboard.

  The Field Manual. A battered book in the wheelhouse holds a page per station, the tackle
  and bait basics, and the Catch Log with its threat dossiers. It fills in as the crew discovers
  things, like Lethal Company's terminal bestiary.


  The crew of six
  Each crewman takes a light role with a small perk, but any hand can still work any station,
  and there are twelve stations for six people, so the crew is always choosing what to leave
  unmanned. A station works only while someone stands at it; walking away from a reel mid-
  fight hands the fish the line.

  Bots fill empty berths if the host chooses. In the lobby the host sets the crew size from 1
  to 6 and whether empty berths get bots. A bot takes a role, works the station it is ordered
  to through the ping wheel ("Take the pump," "Take port rod," "Follow me"), and falls back to
  a sensible default watch when no one gives orders. Bots fish at a competent-but-not-
  expert level, never dive unless ordered, and do not use voice. A dropped player's seat is
  taken by a bot after the 2-minute reconnect window, per the shared arcade rule.

  Bot crew
  A bot is a crewman with a role, an inventory, and a body that can fall overboard, driven by
  a small utility brain in trawl_bots.cpp . It scores every station it could work each second,
  weighted by its role, standing orders, and what is happening, and walks to the best one.
  Bots are good hands, not good captains: they never decide where the boat goes or when
  to go home.

  Skill. The host picks one of three levels for all bots.

     Able               400 ms                      70%                80%            ±2°

     Old Hand           250 ms                      85%                90%            ±1°

  Reactions that override the watch

       Overboard: the nearest bot throws the life ring (with its aim error), the Medic bot runs
       to the rail, and the helm bot stops the screw.
       The bell: every bot moves to the nearest defensive post (cannon, gaff at the rail, rifle if
       carried) and looks for the threat.
       Boarders: bots with gaffs fight; others back away from the rail.
       Leak or fire: the Bosun bot, or the nearest bot if there is no Bosun, goes to it.
       Charmed or grabbed crewman: the nearest bot rings the bell, then helps.

  Orders (ping wheel, aimed at a bot or at a station): take this station; follow me; hold here;
  haul the net; cut the line; fire on my mark; dive (Diver-role bots only); go home (all bots
  stop fishing and secure the deck).

  What bots never do unless ordered: dive, use depth charges or explosive harpoons,
  board derelicts or the Ghost Ship, raise the lantern, or ring the bell for no reason. Bots do
  not use voice; they show short text barks ("Fish on, port!", "Water in the hold!") above their
  heads and in the ping feed. A dead bot's ghost rings the bell when a threat comes within
  10 m.

  moves to the air pump. When something big comes, the rod hands drop everything for the
  cannon and the gaffs.

  Moving on the deck. Top-down, 8-way movement at 3 m/s, slower carrying (a 40 kg tuna
  halves speed; two hands carrying one halves it less). Ladders to the wheelhouse roof, the
  mast, and the engine room. The deck is wet: at more than 12° of roll, unbraced players
  slide; at 25°, they fall. Holding a rail or a station braces.

  Inventory. Four slots per hand (Lethal Company's rule), one of them the hands. Large fish
  and salvage are carried in both hands and can be thrown short distances. Tools: gaff, knife,
  lamp, flare pistol, speargun, rope, and so on.

  Talking. Communication is the core skill, so the game carries it, not a third-party app.

       Proximity voice through the shared arcade layer, the game's main channel: voices
       carry about 12 m on deck and are muffled by walls and wind. Heavy rain and a running
       engine cut the range in half.
       Speaking tube from the wheelhouse to the engine room and to the bow, always clear.
       Walkie (bought): channel-wide voice, one per slot, runs on batteries.
       Ping wheel for players without a microphone: "Fish on," "Need gaff," "Steer port,"
       "Something below," "Get out of the water," and a point marker.
       The ship's bell: anyone can ring it; everybody hears it anywhere on the boat, and the
       dead can ring it too.

  The sonar
  The wheelhouse sonar is a round phosphor scope (the same visual language as the
  expedition sonar chart) and the crew's only view below the surface outside the lantern's
  light.

       Passive (always on): shows only loud things near the boat. At half speed or more, the
       Gannet's own screw drowns it out.
       Active ping: one every 3 s, 150 m radius. Returns show for 6 s and fade. Every ping
       writes noise into the water at the boat, which Ghost Worms and curious species hear.
       Depth dial: the operator picks which depth band the scope shows; a side-scan toggle
       shows the water column under the boat as a profile.
       What returns look like: schools as dotted clouds, single fish as blips sized by size class,
       set gear as bright squares, wrecks as hard shapes, kelp as shadow that hides what is
       behind it, and in the Grotto every return has a faint echo twin. The Kraken whites out
       the whole scope.
       Marking: the operator clicks a contact to mark it; every crewman sees a bearing arrow
       at the edge of their screen for 10 s.
       Second sonar (Slipway upgrade): a second operator, and depth numbers on every
       contact.

  Dock screens
  The dock is walked, not menued: the chalkboard (quota, nights, money, role upgrades),
  the Chandler's counter, the Fish Market scales (fish are carried and weighed one by one,
  or in crates), the Owners' office window (salvage), the Slipway, and the chart table on
  board.


  Boat and sea physics
  The boat, the water, every line, and every fish on a line are one physical system stepped at
  60 Hz on the host ( trawl_boat.cpp ). The rule: anything that pulls on the boat moves the
  boat, and anything that moves the boat moves the crew.

  The sea surface. Height at any point is a sum of four directional waves (a cheap Gerstner
  set) whose amplitude comes from the weather. The top-down view shows swell as moving
  light bands and foam; the side views (dive, reel camera) show the actual profile. A per-
  ground current vector and the wind vector push everything on the surface: the boat,
  floats, the net, corpses, and blood.

  The hull. A rigid body in the plane (x, y, heading) plus three scalar degrees of freedom: roll,
  pitch, and heave. Buoyancy is computed from eight hull sample points against the wave
  height. Weight on board is not a single number: the hold, the ice, every fish on deck, every
  player, the net drum, and water in the bilge each sit at a position, so a full net on the stern
  pitches the bow up and five crew at the port rail during a fight list the boat to port. Roll
  above 12° makes loose items and unbraced players slide; above 35° the boat is on its beam
  and takes water over the rail.

  Damage. The hull has six sections, each with integrity 0–100. Rams, bites, rocks, and
  grinding against a wreck lower it; below 40 a section leaks, below 10 it floods fast. Leaks
  fill the bilge; the pumps empty it. Hull plates (bought) raise section maximums; a hand
  with a patch kit can stop a leak in 6 s while standing in water.

  The engine. Coal into the firebox raises boiler pressure; the telegraph setting draws
  pressure into shaft speed. Pressure in the green gives full thrust; red for more than 5 s
  blows a relief valve (a steam jet that burns anyone in the engine room and stops the screw
  for 20 s). The screw is loud: the engine's noise output feeds the ecosystem's sound layer
  by telegraph setting (stop 0, slow 2, half 5, full 9).

  Lines, nets, and ropes. Every line (rod lines, the harpoon tether, the trawl warps, the
  diver's air hose, the life-ring rope, the anchor) is a Verlet chain with 8–24 segments,
  reusing the Chain code from the master reference. Tension is read from the constraint

  error at the anchor end. When tension exceeds the line's rated strength for longer than its
  shock window (0.2 s for mono, 0.5 s for braid, 1 s for steel), it snaps. Lines can foul each

  Light. The lantern mast, deck lamps, hand lamps, flares, and bioluminescence write into a
  light grid shared with the ecosystem (a new light layer, see The food web). The top-down
  view is dark except where light reaches; the sea beyond the lantern radius is drawn black
  with only foam and glints. Lantern levels: hooded (4 m), low (8 m), full (14 m), searchlight
  (a 30 m cone the operator aims).

  Moon. Full moon doubles plankton at the surface (more bait fish, more predators up high);
  new moon brings deep species shallower. The phase advances one step per night, so the
  three nights of a deadline differ.


  Fishing
  Fishing is a physics fight between a player, a rod, a line, and an animal that is also an agent
  in the food web. The UI shows only what an angler would feel: rod bend, line angle, reel
  sound, and a tension gauge on the reel. There is no minigame bar.

  Bait and lures
  Every species has a diet in the food web, so bait is the food web in the player's hands. A
  fish takes what it eats.

       Dead bait: squid strips, shrimp, chunks cut from caught fish (a tuna chunk catches
       sharks). Loses scent over 3 minutes.
       Live bait: bait fish caught on handlines and kept in the bait well. Swims on the hook,
       draws predators by movement and by scent, and can itself be eaten by something
       bigger than what you wanted.
       Lures: spoon (flash, mid-water), jig (vertical, deep), popper (surface, loud), glow lure
       (light), rattle (sound), plug (dives to a set depth). Each has a flash, noise, and scent
       profile the species' senses score.
       Chum: offal from the gutting table or a bought chum bucket. Writes blood into the
       scent grid. Brings fish fast, and brings whatever eats them.

  The cast
  Hold to charge, release to cast; aim with the mouse. Wind drifts the lure, and a cross-wind
  cast lands off target. The lure sinks at its own rate; the angler counts depth by the line
  counter on the reel. Retrieve style is the player's input: steady reel, jig (tap), twitch-and-
  pause, or dead drift. Each species scores the presentation against its preference; a
  matched presentation roughly triples the bite chance.


  The bite
  A fish that decides to take goes through inspect (the rod tip ticks), nibble (small taps, 1–
  4), and take (the rod loads). Setting the hook during the take (a 250 ms window, 150 ms for
  wary species) hooks it; striking on a nibble spooks it and teaches that school to avoid that
  lure for 2 minutes. Circle hooks set themselves if the player simply reels.


  The fight
  The hooked fish is an agent with strength, stamina, and a fight pattern from its species
  record: runs, deep dives, jumps and head-shakes, circling under the boat, sounding into
  cover (a wreck or kelp, where the line chafes), or rolling (sharks, which wrap the line).

  The angler controls three things:

   1. Reel (hold): gains line only when tension is below drag.
   2. Rod angle (mouse direction relative to the fish): pulling against the direction of the run
      tires the fish fastest (side pressure); pulling straight back wastes stamina; a high rod
      angle while it jumps throws the hook.
   3. Drag (scroll): how much tension slips line instead of breaking it. Low drag is safe but
      lets the fish run into cover; high drag ends fights fast and snaps lines.

  Tension is computed from the chain physics, not a formula for the UI. It breaks lines over
  their rating; below 5% for 1.5 s (slack) lets barbless or circle hooks fall out. A fish's pull is a
  real force on the boat: a 200 kg marlin can tow the Gannet at slow speed, and the helm
  can help by backing the boat down on it.


  Fight tuning
  Every hooked fish runs one model, shaped by its fight pattern. All constants live in
  trawl_data.cpp ; these are the starting values --trawl-fight is tuned against.

  The model




                                                                                               Page 16 of 50
The Trawl — Arcade Game 2 Design Document (Draft)




       Pull: a fish's pull in kilograms-force = its weight × the pattern's pull ratio, rising to 1.5×
       during a burst.
       Stamina: seconds of full pull = 20 + 6 × √weight (a 4 kg snapper has 32 s; a 200 kg
       marlin has 105 s).
       Drain: stamina drops by (line tension ÷ pull) per second. Side pressure (rod angle
       within 45° of square to the fish's heading, pulling against its turn) multiplies drain by
       1.5; pulling straight back by 0.6; slack drains nothing.
       Recovery: when tension falls under 20% of pull, the fish recovers 0.3 s of stamina per
       second, so a timid angler never ends a fight.
       Landing: at under 15% stamina the fish comes alongside and can be gaffed, netted, or
       lifted; it bursts once more if the gaff misses.
       Passing the rod: a crewman can take over a rod with E; the handover gives 1 s of held
       tension, no slack.

  Landing
       Fish under 5 kg: lifted over the rail.
       5–40 kg: a second hand with the gaff or the landing net. Gaffing has a timing window as
       the fish comes alongside; a miss spooks it into one more run.
       40 kg and over: tail rope, then the net winch or the cannon's winch hauls it aboard, and
       the whole boat lists while it comes up.

  A landed fish flops on deck (a physics body that slides with the roll and can flop back
  overboard) until someone takes it to the gutting table.


  The trawl
  The net is the big-money, big-risk tool. Shooting it takes 15 s; while down it is a Verlet mesh
  of 8×6 nodes towed on two warps. It fills by sweeping through schools (catch rate =
  school density × mouth width × speed), gets heavier, and drags the stern down and slows
  the boat. It catches everything in its path, including what the crew didn't want: bycatch
  jellies sting whoever empties the net, and a sleeping Ghost Worm in the mouth turns the
  haul into a fight. It snags on wrecks and reefs (warps shriek, the boat slews) and must be
  backed off or cut loose, losing the net. Hauling needs a hand on the winch and a hand
  guiding the boom. A full net's cod end is emptied onto the sorting deck in one wet,
  flopping pile.

  Set gear
       Longline: 20 baited hooks on a line with a radio buoy at each end, set and left to fish.
       Returning finds fish, empty hooks, or fish heads: other predators strip hooked catch
       (depredation), which tells you what is feeding there.
       Pots: crab and lobster pots dropped on a reef or wreck with a float, hauled on a later
       pass.
       Set gear drifts with the current and can be stolen by the Ghost Ship's crew.


  Shooting
  Guns are the fast, loud, bloody way to take fish and the only way to stop some threats.
  Every shot trades value and safety for speed: a shot fish is worth less than a hooked one,
  it bleeds, and the report carries through the water.

  Ballistics. Projectiles are simulated, not hitscan. Above the surface: gravity and light drag.
  At the surface: a round's velocity drops to 10% in the first 0.3 m and it stops within 1.5 m,
  so rifles only work on fish at or breaking the surface. Harpoons and spears keep their
  energy far longer in water. Refraction: a target seen below the surface is drawn where the
  light says it is, 15–25% nearer and shallower than it really is, so aiming at a fish in the water
  means aiming below it. Hits resolve against the fish's body (head, body, tail); a head hit on
  a fish under its size class kills clean.

  The tethered harpoon. A harpoon hit makes the target a hooked fish (see Fishing) with

  wounded fish that escapes bleeds into the scent grid as it goes.

  Noise and blood. Each shot writes its noise value into the sound layer at the muzzle and
  again at the impact point. Fearful species scatter; curious species come; aggressive ones
  come fast. Every wound writes blood (size × wound fraction × 2 per second, size × 20 on
  death). A depth charge is a dinner bell for a whole ground: it is the fastest money in the
  game and the fastest way to raise Wake (see The food web).

  Friendly fire is on. A rifle round or a harpoon can hit a crewman, and a harpoon tether can
  drag one over the rail.


  The food web
  Every ground runs one ecosystem simulation on the engine code Red Tide already has
  (agents, the scent and sound grids, the species file loader). The Trawl reuses that code to
  save time, not its design: the rules, numbers, species, and tuning here are written for a
  boat on the surface at night. The Trawl's web has seven layers (diet, blood and scent,
  sound, territory, symbiosis, flora, and the crew) plus three that exist only in this game:
  light, vibration, and Wake. The crew is a species in the web, so everything they catch, spill,
  and shine changes what comes next.

  What each ground holds
  About 25 fish and invertebrate species, 6 threats, and 4–6 flora per ground, as records in
  trawl_species.json using the shared species file format (class, size 1–6, trophic tier,
  diet, blood yield and threshold, senses, temperament, home, social). The Trawl adds four
  fields: depth band (surface, upper, mid, deep, abyss, with a night shift: many species rise
  after dark), light response (drawn, neutral, or shy, by colour), bite profile (preferred bait,
  lure flash, noise, and retrieve), and fight pattern (runs, dives, jumps, circles, sounds into
  cover, rolls).

  Red Tide: shared assets and code, separate games. Red Tide and the Trawl are different
  games. They share only what saves time: the ecosystem engine code, the species file
  loader, and, where the same animal appears in both (a Great White, a barracuda), its sprite
  sheet and sound cues as a starting point to re-colour and re-pose. Nothing about design
  carries over. Every Trawl species has its own record, numbers, and behaviour written for
  this game: fish are animals you fight on a line and read from above through sonar and light,
  not beasts a diver shoots.

  The three new layers
   1. Light. The light grid from the boat physics feeds the ecosystem. Plankton and forage
      fish rise toward light; predators follow the forage; light-shy deep species retreat from
      it; curious threats (Anglers, Sirens, Drowned sailors) are drawn to it. A full lantern
      roughly doubles bites after 30 minutes by pulling a whole food chain up under the hull.
       Red light is invisible to most deep species.
   2. Vibration. Taut lines, the trawl, the screw, a thrashing hooked fish, and hull impacts
      write vibration into the water. Ghost Worms, sharks, and anything with a lateral-line
      sense track it. A fish fought for five minutes is a beacon.
   3. Wake. A per-ground scalar (0–100) that sums what the crew has put into the water:
      blood above the ambient level, loud noise, bright light, cursed salvage, the Pale Eye's
      gaze, and depth charges (+15 each). It decays 1 point a minute when the crew is quiet
       and dark. Wake is what calls the big three (see Threats) and it is shown to the crew
       only indirectly: the music, the fish going quiet, the sonar's clutter, and the ghosts'
       outlines.


  How the web plays
       Bait chains. Handline silversides, live-bait them for mahi, cut the mahi for shark
       chunks. Each step up the chain is worth more and bleeds more.
       Depredation. A hooked fish is prey. A tuna on a line for two minutes in the Weeds has a
       real chance of coming up as a head; a barracuda pack strips a longline. What took the
       fish tells the crew what is under them.
       Satiation. A predator that has just eaten ignores bait for a while. A Great White that
       took a tuna off the line gives the crew a few quiet minutes; a hungry one follows the
       boat.
       Schools. Forage fish move as boids; predators herd them into bait balls near the
       surface, which the sonar shows and which surface feeders and gulls give away. Netting
       through a bait ball is the best trawl of the night, and every predator around it is already
       there.
       Cascades across nights. Because a ground remembers across a deadline, heavy
       netting of forage fish on night one leaves predators hungry on night two (more bites,
       more bold approaches, more depredation), and killing the Weeds' sea otters lets
       urchins strip the kelp, emptying the canopy by night three.
       Bycatch and discards. Everything the net brings up that isn't sold goes back over the
       side dead, as blood and food, feeding scavengers and raising Wake. Sorting quickly and
       returning bycatch alive (a hand at the sorting chute) keeps the water quieter.

       Blood rules. Blood diffuses to neighbouring cells each second and decays 3% per
       second in open water (1% in the Grotto's still water) with the current carrying it
       downstream, so a slick trails away from the boat and predators arrive from down-
       current.

  crew and prints populations, blood, Wake, and threat arrivals. A ground with no crew must
  stay stable (no species extinct and none above 150% of its start) for three simulated
  nights.

  The Weeds
  A kelp forest whose canopy reaches the surface in dark golden mats, with clear lanes
  between. Everything big lives at the edges.

       Catch. Bait: anchovy balls, kelp perch. Mid: kelp bass, sheephead, rockfish, bonito.
       Edge runs: bluefin tuna (a 20-minute fight on the heavy rod), yellowtail. Bottom:
       halibut, abalone (by diving).
       Flora. Kelp (shelters fish, blocks sonar, fouls lines), urchin barrens (where kelp has
       been grazed away; empty and exposed), sea otter rafts that eat urchins and keep the
       kelp standing.
       Signature: kelp fouls everything. Running the engine through the canopy wraps the
       screw (speed halves until a hand cuts it free from the stern ladder, in the water). Nets
       fill with kelp that weighs as much as fish and is worth nothing. Hooked fish run into the
       canopy to chafe the line.
       Threats. Sirens (sing from the kelp lanes, pull the helm toward the rocks), Kelp Wraiths
       (drift unseen in the canopy, entangle a crewman at the rail), a Great White that patrols
       the seaward edge, Feral Mermen who cut nets.


  The Grotto (the Cave)
  The Gannet sails through a sea arch into a flooded cave the size of a cathedral: black
  water, dripping stalactites, glowing mould on the walls, and the hulls of smugglers' ships

       Catch. Blind cave fish, cave shrimp (bait), glass eels, lantern fish, cave cod, giant

  The big three
  Each of the three Wake-called threats runs a short script on top of its food-web brain.
  Every one of them can be escaped, survived, or killed, and each takes a different kind of
  crew work. For reference, the Gannet makes 1.5 m/s at slow, 3 at half, and 4.5 at full (5.9
  with the compound engine).

  The Great White (Lagoon at deadline 2+, Weeds, Atlantis)

   1. Investigate (30–60 s). A fin circles at the lantern's edge, about 20 m out. It takes any
       hooked fish it finds and bumps the hull once (−5). If blood near the boat falls below 60
       and nobody is in the water, it leaves after 90 s.
   2. Hunt. Starts if it is wounded, if a crewman is in the water, or if blood stays high. Every
       20–30 s it drops out of sight (the sonar shows it rising, 2 s of warning) and rams: −25 to
       a section, 15° of roll, and unbraced crew at that rail go over. A crewman in the water is
       its first target, at 8 m/s.
   3. Break-off. At 30% HP it flees, or it leaves once sated (60 kg eaten, a stolen tuna
       counts). Harpooned and tethered, it becomes a Run-pattern fight on the cannon
       winch; a landed White (300–700 kg) pays well and bleeds the whole ground.

  The Ghost Ship (Grotto, Atlantis)

   1. The answer. At Wake 65 on a moonless night, or after the ship's bell has been rung
       more than 10 times in one night, a bell answers from the dark. Fog thickens; the sonar
       shows a hull 300 m out.




                                                                                                                   Page 37 of 50
The Trawl — Arcade Game 2 Design Document (Draft)




   2. Approach (about 90 s). It closes at 3.5 m/s, so full steam outruns it and half steam does
       not. Fleeing is a real option.
   3. Alongside. It throws three grapples (each cut in 3 s with a knife) and boards Drowned
      sailors three at a time every 20 s, up to 6–10.
   4. Ending it. Either a crewman crosses to the Ghost Ship and rings its own bell (the
       boarders fall into the sea), or the crew deals 1,200 hull damage (explosive harpoons
       and depth charges under it). Either way it sinks and leaves a floating relic chest: two
       relics and 300–600 in salvage.
   5. If ignored. After 4 minutes alongside it casts off with any set gear and whatever un-
      iced catch its boarders carried.

  The Kraken (Atlantis only)

   1. Tells (60 s). The Glass: the wind dies and the sea goes flat. Fish vanish from the sonar.
      For the last 20 s the scope is white.
   2. Arms (0–60 s). Two arms rise every 15 s, up to four. Each picks a target in order: the net,
       a crewman at the rail, the lantern mast, loose gear. A grabbed crewman is carried off in
       4 s unless the arm takes 80 damage (knife 20 a hit, rifle 25, harpoon 120).
   3. Hold (60–90 s). An arm on the warps drags the stern down; cutting the net loose buys
       30 s. If the lantern is dark and the screw runs at full for 45 s straight, it loses the boat
       and sinks away.
   4. Crush (90 s on). The remaining arms wrap the hull: −30 to a section every 10 s. Three
       sections at 0 sinks the Gannet. Every 30 s the body surfaces for 6 s and the eye (weak
       point) is open.
   5. Ending it. Escape by darkness, speed, and cutting the net; survive by severing arms
       until it gives up (all eight arms cut); or kill it through the eye (3,000 HP, realistically only
       with explosive harpoons and depth charges). A kill leaves the Kraken's beak (a 2,000
       relic) and ends every threat on the ground for the rest of the night.


  Diving and salvage
  Diving is The Trawl's Lethal Company facility run: one or two crew go down into a wreck
  for salvage while the deck keeps them alive. It is the highest-value, highest-trust activity
  in the game.

  Gear. The hardhat suit (bought, 350) and later the diving bell (boat upgrade, 1,200). The
  hardhat diver is on an air hose from the deck pump and a lifeline to the gantry winch; the
  bell carries two divers to 120 m and gives them a pocket of air to return to.

  The view. Diving switches that player to a side-view pixel scene built on the parkour side's
  movement code (walk, climb, squeeze, no jumping hazards), lit only by the helmet lamp.
  The deck crew sees the diver as a dot on sonar and hears them on the speaking tube.

       Air. A hand works the pump (a rhythm input; the gauge must stay in the green). Stop

       Worms, Barracuda), and snag if the boat drifts. The helm has to hold station over the
       wreck against wind and current.
       Recall. Two sharp tugs from the diver (a key) or the deck means "haul me up". The
       winch brings a diver up at 1 m/s; faster risks the Bends (slowed and dizzy for the rest of
       the night).

  Wrecks. Each ground has 2–4 procedurally laid-out wrecks (smuggler hulls, whalers,
  Atlantean terraces) with rooms, flooded corridors, locked cabins (crowbar), and things
  living in them. Salvage items are physical: heavy, carried in both hands, sometimes two-
  diver lifts, sent up in the salvage basket on its own line.

  Layout rules

   1. Start from a hull profile for the type and cut it into decks, then rooms on a 4 × 3 m cell
       grid joined by doors, hatches, and breaches. Some hulls lie on their side (30%), turning
       ladders into floors and floors into walls.
   2. Every room must be reachable within the hardhat's 40 m of hose from the entry point,
       or from the bell for bell wrecks.
   3. About 30% of wrecks have a squeeze passage (one diver, no carried salvage through it)
      that shortcuts to the richest room.
   4. Place one air pocket per five rooms.
   5. Spread the salvage budget with heavier and more valuable items deeper in; 40% of the
      value sits behind locked doors (crowbar or the Diver's Wreck Rat).
   6. Silt on every floor: moving fast raises it and blinds the helmet lamp for 5 s.
   7. Roll residents from the ground's roster: morays, octopus, isopods, congers, a Ghost
       Worm hatchling (Grotto, Atlantis), and one Drowned sailor per four rooms in the Grotto
       and Atlantis. Atlantis wrecks have a 10% chance per cabin of a cursed idol.

   --trawl-wreck <ground> <seed> prints a wreck as ASCII and asserts reachability, the
  hose limit, and the salvage budget.

  What lives in wrecks. Moray eels (bite and hold), octopus (steal the lamp), isopods,
  sleeping Ghost Worm hatchlings, and Drowned sailors who were only waiting for
  company.



  Economy and progression
  Money is shillings. Fish pay now and spoil; salvage keeps and pays best on deadline day.
  That split is the economy's main decision.

  Fish value = species base price × weight × grade × freshness × glut.

       Grade is how it was taken (hook 100%, spear 85%, harpoon 75%, rifle 70%, depth
       charge 50%, explosive 30%), minus 10% for every bite taken out of it on the line.
       Freshness starts at 100% and falls 1% per real minute on deck, 0.2% per minute gutted
       and iced. Fish kept overnight in ice lose 25%; un-iced fish rot and sell for nothing.
       Glut. Each species' price at the Fish Market drops 3% for every 10 kg of it sold that
       deadline, so six hundred kilos of sardines is not a strategy.

  The Catch Log. Every species caught is logged with its heaviest weight, and every threat

  Trawl never pays into the main game's economy.


  Death, injury, and ghosts
  Most deaths start with a crewman in the water, so the rescue is a mechanic, not a
  cutscene.

  Overboard. A player in the water sees a side view of themselves treading water, has 25 s
  before cold and exhaustion drown them (12 s in a storm), and is a bleeding, splashing,
  noisy thing in the food web. The deck can throw the life ring (a thrown Verlet rope; aim for
  the swimmer, then haul), steer the boat alongside (the screw kills swimmers, so the helm
  must stop it), or send a second crewman in on a line. Things that took a crewman over the
  rail usually aren't done with them.

  Injuries (no health bar). Bites, burns, and falls cause injuries that change what a player
  can do until the dock: a hooked hand (can't reel), a broken arm (one-handed only, can't
  carry big fish), a burn (slower), and a bite (bleeds into the scent grid until bandaged). A
  second serious injury in one night is death.

  Death. A dead crewman is dead for the night. If their body stays aboard, they revive at the
  dock. If the body is lost to the sea, the Owners deduct 8% of the crew's money for the
  replacement hand (Lethal Company's rule), and the crewman still returns at the dock.

  Ghosts. The dead walk the deck as translucent figures who cannot touch anything except
  three things: the ship's bell, the sonar screen (read only), and the ghost's own lantern, a
  faint cold light that only other ghosts can see. Ghosts see threats in the dark within 10 m
  as pale outlines for a moment after they move, so a dead crewman becomes the best
  lookout on the boat. They speak only to other ghosts and through the bell.

  Total loss. If every crewman dies, or the boat sinks, the night's catch is lost, the Owners
  charge 25% of money for salvaging the Gannet, and the next night starts at the dock. The
  run continues unless the quota can no longer be met.

  The water column. Red Tide's scent and sound grids are 3D at 2 m cells. The Trawl uses a 4
  m horizontal grid with five depth bands (surface 0–2 m, upper 2–15, mid 15–60, deep 60–
  200, abyss 200+); agents keep a continuous depth for fishing and diving. Species records
  gain a depth band, a light preference, and a fight pattern.

  Networking. Host-authoritative, 6 players, through arcade_net.cpp . Snapshots at 20 Hz;
  state is filtered by what each client can know: fish below the surface are sent only inside
  the light radius, on a client's sonar ping, or while hooked. The rod holder's tension gauge is

The Trawl — Arcade Game 2 Design Document (Draft)




  predicted locally from the chain physics and reconciled with the host so reeling feels
  immediate at 150 ms of latency. Voice uses the arcade layer's voice channel (Opus-
  encoded, positional), with the host mixing nothing: clients attenuate by distance and walls
  themselves. --net-loop trawl runs a host and five bot clients over loopback.

  Performance budget. About 250 active agents within 150 m of the boat at full rate (10 Hz
  decisions, 60 Hz movement); population counters beyond. Up to 16 Verlet chains at once
  (6 rod lines, 2 warps, the net mesh counted as one, hose, lifeline, tether, life ring, anchor,
  longlines).

  Art. Top-down pixel deck at 2× the parkour pixel scale; the sea is drawn black outside the
  light grid with foam and glint pixels; fish show as shadows until lit or landed, then as full
  sprites; threats get the parkour beast sprite pipeline. Side views (dive, overboard, the reel
  camera on a big fight) use the parkour renderer. The porthole bezel frames everything, per
  the arcade's shared frame.

  Sound. Every cue goes through the master reference's cue registry and buses. Key cues:
  reel ratchet pitched by tension, the line's hum rising before a snap, the drag's zip, the
  winch, rod creak, the pump's rhythm and the diver's breathing, the bell, the hull mic's
  knocks, each threat's tell. The music is a drone and a shanty fragment that stops when
  anything with Wake-level size is within 60 m: the silence is the first tell.



  Sound design
  In the Trawl, sound is information: most threats are heard before they are seen, and the
  music going quiet is the first warning. Everything is synthesized through the master
  reference's cue registry and buses; where a Red Tide cue fits the same animal, it is copied
  as a starting point and re-tuned.

  Voice
  Proximity voice is processed in the world: muffled behind walls, thinned by wind and rain,
  lost under the engine at full, crackling on walkies, and bubbling for a crewman in the water.
  Ghosts hear each other with a cold reverb and hear the living faintly. Every sound tell has
  a visual cue for players who play without sound, and a subtitle toggle shows bot barks and
  telegraph messages as text.

  Definition of Done

       Six players over the internet complete a deadline including a dive, a Kraken encounter,
